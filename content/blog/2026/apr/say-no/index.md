In my first years in the software industry, I used to say *"nothing is impossible, it is just a matter of time"* (cliché, I know). This is "fun" at first, but it leads to distractions, team friction and that usual feature that no one wants to maintain but that now will forever exist. I am writing this as a reminder to my future self and as a guide for those early in their careers on why "no" is often the most productive word in your vocabulary.

## **"Yes" will hurt your team**

Product and Engineering managers tend to rely on software engineers to understand how feasible a task is. This is why teams tend to plan and agree on a plan for the next delivery cycle.

But, there will always be "external forces" that will bring ad-hoc requests. Sometimes a bug fix or small feature, other times a discovery in a particular tech/area. In the minds of many, those are small enough to be done on the side and some of us can't resist the temptation to say *"yes"*. Also, who doesn't want to look good by delivering so much?

The problem with those is that they can quickly grow in scope and consume a lot of your or the team's time, or even be deemed low priority / won't do. In the meantime, your team's plan gets affected and can put a deliverable at risk.

## **How to decide?**

I am not saying never say "yes", because there will be many situations when you need to be proactive and do things without asking. It is the usual "it depends", but there are some points to consider before accepting unplanned work.

1. **Is this an ad-hoc tweak or bug fix?** Small requests are rarely small. Ask yourself whether it needs to be done now or just tracked — when in doubt, align with the team, since the answer is usually "it can wait".
2. **Is this a full blown feature or project?** Every line of code you write today is a liability your team carries tomorrow. If this project or feature is not well planned, you might be stuck supporting it forever (the worst is when users love it but it is a massive tech debt pile). If your team does not have a plan for it, most likely it should be placed in the backlog for proper review.
3. **Is this a discovery request?** Unless you are a Senior+ engineer, which usually does plenty of high-level research, deep-diving into a new discovery initiative on a whim is a distraction. It will be unrelated to your team's current plan and you should talk with your lead before jumping in.
4. **Is this an incident?** Critical outages always take priority, but being the "forever fixer" isn't great. Sometimes, the best way to say "no" is to observe rather than act, since only that way the team will be able to learn as well. Also, you will thank me when you go on holiday without being paged ;)
5. **Is this task better done by someone else?** Sometimes we say "yes" because we can do it, not because we should be the ones doing it. Someone might want to upskill in that area, or perhaps it is something another team has ownership of and you might be crossing a boundary (albeit I am a believer that engineers should do cross-boundary work). Just align with the right people before committing to anything.

These points become even more important in times where AI makes it easier than ever to take on more work, sometimes even encouraged.

## **The AI \~effect**

AI makes it easier than ever to take on tasks that we wouldn't have before. After all, we all know the hype is about how people can be X times more productive with AI, so we must be on top of our game and do more when possible (source: most of today's LinkedIn posts). Engineers can easily justify Items 1 to 3 from the above list because AI makes it easy to do them.

The issue is that everything generated by AI needs someone to review it very carefully. We have all probably seen what happens when AI output is not properly reviewed, but in case you haven't, [Amazon had bad outages recently due to it](https://arstechnica.com/ai/2026/03/after-outages-amazon-to-make-senior-engineers-sign-off-on-ai-assisted-changes/) and [other companies as well](https://www.theregister.com/2026/02/27/lovable_app_vulnerabilities/) (Lovable).

Considering those would have been unplanned tasks, your team can get overwhelmed with the amount of reviewing (even if it is not slop) and context switching around it. Worse, many times those will not correlate to a vision / goal the team has defined for that cycle, making it even more stressful for those involved.

For planned tasks or for projects with continuous and defined flow of tasks, AI can indeed unlock a team's productivity, albeit with the similar bottlenecks around code review. AI makes it even easier to say "yes" to unplanned work, which means the discipline around saying "no" matters more, not less.

## **Should I never say "yes" then?**

As you get more senior, you will learn that all of this is very nuanced and even senior engineers sometimes make these misjudgments. There will be moments that you will be expected to be proactive and tackle something independently from the plan, such as starting research for a new strategic initiative or an adhoc request that is critical to the business, so treat this as a guidance not as gospel.

Eventually you will develop a "gut feeling" for those tasks that should not be immediately accepted and instead push back on them. It's just pattern recognition from all the times you said yes when you shouldn't have. Save yourself some scar tissue and learn how to say "no" at the right moments.
